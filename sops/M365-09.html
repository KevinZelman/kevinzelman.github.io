M365-09 · Teams Phone, Desk Phone & Voicemail Standard

Who gets a number, how a desk phone is re-registered, and where an emergency call says you are. Written because a request with no procedure has no owner.

Prerequisites This document names four things that must exist before it can fully operate — the DID register, the emergency location map, the resource account inventory and the firmware baseline. They are numbered with owners in Appendix B. Two of them probably do not exist today, and the document says so rather than assuming them.

1. Purpose — one ticket, ninety days, no runbook

A common story: a request to re-register a Teams desk phone reaches its service target, and three months later it is still open and still in violation. It is not a difficult request. It is a request that nobody owned, because there was no procedure that said who does it or how, and a request with no procedure has no owner. A request with no owner ages.

That is the whole reason this document exists, and it is worth stating in §1 rather than dressing it up as a governance initiative. In many small organizations the telephony estate is small, it is not complicated, and it has no written standard at all — so every phone question is answered from first principles by whoever is available, and the ones that arrive when nobody is available simply wait.

The escalation machinery around a ticket like that works perfectly and achieves nothing. It re-notifies hourly. Alerting a person repeatedly about work they have no procedure for is not escalation; it is noise with a timestamp. Section 4 is the runbook that closes the desk-phone ticket, and the first test of it is closing a real one.

2. Who gets a number, and who does not

A telephone number is not a piece of configuration. It is an entitlement with a licence behind it and a recurring cost attached (M365-20), and it should be granted for a reason rather than by default.

Population Number? Basis
Staff whose role requires being reachable by people outside the organization on a published number Yes — a personal DID. Front desk, Finance, Security, Facilities, senior staff, anybody printed on a business card or a web page.
A departmental function several people cover No personal numbers — one published number into a call queue (§6). A queue is what the caller actually wants. Five personal numbers in a rota means four of them ring an empty desk.
Staff whose external contact is by email and scheduled meetings No DID by default. Teams-to-Teams calling works without one, and internal calling is the majority of use. A DID nobody has published rings only wrong numbers.
Staff with published office hours or clients who call Yes, on request, with a stated reason. A real case, and a small population. The reason takes one line and makes the annual review possible.
Temporary workers No. They answer the department’s queue. ACC-09. An entitlement that ends at the end of the engagement is an entitlement nobody remembers to end.
A lobby, lab, meeting room, elevator or corridor Yes — a common-area device with its own number (§9). Its physical location is the point of it, and that location is its emergency address (§8).

The assumption worth examining before the entitlement list is set

The usual starting position is that everybody who had a handset on the old system gets a number on the new one. That is a migration artefact, not a decision — it carries forward an entitlement nobody in the room ever chose, along with its recurring cost, and it makes §3 and §8 larger than they need to be for ever.

The organization should re-decide it once, deliberately, against the table above, and then hold the line. The number of people who genuinely need a published external number is smaller than the number who currently have one at almost every organization.

ACTION: Tier 4 to confirm the entitlement classes, with the cost attached (M365-20 §7). This is a five-minute decision that is never made because nobody puts it in front of anyone.

3. Number assignment and the DID register

Be honest about the starting point: the register probably does not exist.

IT does not currently hold a single authoritative record of the organization’s DID ranges, which numbers are assigned, to whom, which are held by resource accounts, which are reserved, and what emergency address each carries. What exists is spread across the carrier’s portal, the Teams admin centre, a spreadsheet or two, and institutional memory.

Nothing else in this document works properly without it. Section 8 in particular is unimplementable without it, because an emergency address is registered per number and there is no way to audit addresses for numbers you cannot list.

Building it is prerequisite 1 in Appendix B, it has a named owner, and it is perhaps a day of work. It is the highest-value day in this document.

3.1 What the register holds

Field Why it is there
Number, in full E.164 form The key. One row per number, including the ones that are unassigned.
Type User / call queue / auto attendant / common area / resource account / reserved / spare.
Assigned to A named person, or the named resource account (ACC-08). Never “Front Office”.
Department, and the owner where it is a queue or an attendant So that §6 has somebody to ask.
Emergency address of record The civic address associated with the number. §8 depends entirely on this column.
Assigned date, and unassigned date Which makes the §10 quarantine visible rather than remembered.
Status Assigned / quarantined / spare / reserved / porting.
Carrier and porting reference Where the number came from and what it would take to move it.

3.2 Assignment rules

  • Numbers are assigned by Tier 2 from the register, never picked ad hoc from the available pool. A number allocated outside the register is a number the organization will lose track of within a year.
  • A published number — anything printed, on a web page, or in the directory — belongs to a function, and is held by a resource account or a queue rather than by a person (§6, §10).
  • Vanity or sequential requests are declined politely. Numbers are a shared resource and a block held for one department is a block unavailable to everyone.
  • Every assignment sets the emergency address in the same action (§8). An assignment that leaves that field blank is not finished.
  • The register is reconciled against the Teams admin centre and the carrier quarterly. Three sources of truth is none.

4. Desk phone re-registration — the runbook

This is the centre of the document and the thing the desk-phone ticket needed. A Teams desk phone signs in with the user’s own Microsoft Entra identity, which means it is an identity device before it is a telephone — and almost every failure below is an identity or a licensing failure wearing a telephone’s clothing.

4.1 When re-registration is needed

  • The phone was signed out, deliberately or otherwise, and shows a sign-in screen.
  • The user’s password was reset, their MFA methods were re-registered, or their sessions were revoked (ACC-03, ACC-05) — all of which invalidate the phone’s sign-in.
  • The device’s refresh token expired, typically after a long period switched off or off the network.
  • The phone was factory reset, reassigned to a different person, or moved between buildings.
  • A tenant change — a new Conditional Access policy, a change of sign-in method — stopped it authenticating. This is the cause people never suspect and it is the one to hold in mind at step 7.

4.2 Sign-in methods

Method How it works When to use it
Web sign-in with a device code The phone displays a short code and a URL. The USER opens that URL on a computer, signs in with their own credentials and completes their own MFA, and the phone registers. The normal method for a personal desk phone. Use it by default.
Username and password on the device Typed directly on the handset. Supported on some models and increasingly not viable — it cannot satisfy modern MFA requirements. Do not plan around it.
Remote sign-in from the Teams admin centre A sign-in is pushed to a device that is already enrolled and visible in the admin centre. The efficient route for a managed estate, and it requires the device to be enrolled — which is prerequisite 3 in Appendix B.
Resource account sign-in The device signs in as a resource account rather than as a person (ACC-08). Common-area and shared phones only (§9). Never a person’s account on a shared device.

IT does not sign in as the user. Ever.

The device-code flow exists precisely so that the user completes their own authentication. A technician who asks for a password to get a phone working has done the thing the organization tells every member of staff nobody from IT will ever do (ACC-15 §4), and has undone a control that took years to establish.

If the user is not available, the phone waits. It is a telephone.

4.3 The runbook

  1. Establish what is actually being reported. “The phone does not work” covers at least four distinct faults: it shows a sign-in screen, it shows the wrong person’s name, it is signed in but takes no calls, or it is dead. Ask, and write the answer in the ticket.
  2. CHECK THE ACCOUNT BEFORE TOUCHING THE PHONE. Is the user licensed for Teams Phone, do they have a number assigned, and is enterprise voice enabled? A phone that will not register because a licence was reclaimed looks exactly like a hardware fault and is diagnosed as one for an hour (M365-20).
  3. Check the network. The phone has an IP address, reaches DNS and the internet, and — where it is powered over Ethernet — the switch port is delivering power. A phone on a port that was re-patched during other work is a common and entirely undramatic cause.
  4. RECORD THE FIRMWARE VERSION NOW, before changing anything. Old firmware is the second most common cause of a phone that will not register, and it is the one nobody writes down, so nobody knows whether the estate is current.
  5. Sign out cleanly on the device before signing in again. Signing in over a stale session is how a phone ends up half-registered, showing the previous user’s name and taking nobody’s calls.
  6. Generate the device code and have the USER complete the web sign-in with their own credentials and their own MFA (§4.2). Stay with them; the flow times out and the code expires.
  7. IF THE CODE IS REJECTED OR THE SIGN-IN LOOPS, stop retrying. Two things cause this and neither is the phone: a Conditional Access policy blocking the device-code authentication flow, or the account being in scope of an MFA requirement the handset cannot satisfy. Both are Tier 3. A rushed phishing-resistant MFA rollout is a classic way to leave users stuck in an MFA setup loop, so this failure mode is common.
  8. Once signed in, update the firmware, then restart, then re-test. Updating after a successful sign-in rather than before means a failed update does not leave an unregistered phone.
  9. Test properly, in this order: internal call in, internal call out, EXTERNAL call in on the DID, external call out, hold, transfer, and voicemail. A phone tested only by dialling a colleague is a phone half-tested.
  10. CONFIRM THE EMERGENCY ADDRESS on the device and in the register (§8). A phone that has moved desk, floor or building has moved emergency address, and this is the step that gets skipped because the phone is now working and everyone is relieved.
  11. Update the DID register and the device record: firmware version, sign-in date, location, emergency address.
  12. Close the ticket with the CAUSE recorded, not “resolved”. The cause column of §4.4 is built from those entries, and without them the next occurrence starts from nothing again — which is how the desk-phone ticket happened (GOV-02).

4.4 Common failure modes, in the order they actually occur

Symptom Most likely cause Fix Owner
Phone sits on the sign-in screen after a password reset The sign-in was invalidated with the password change. Entirely expected and not a fault. Re-register per §4.3. Add it to the ACC-04 reset script so the user is told to expect it. Tier 1
Device code is rejected or the sign-in loops Conditional Access blocking the device-code flow, or an MFA requirement the handset cannot meet. Escalate to Tier 3. Do not keep retrying; each attempt looks identical and tells you nothing. Tier 3
Registers, then drops within hours or days Old firmware, or a token lifetime the device cannot renew. Update firmware first, then re-register. If it recurs on current firmware, Tier 3. Tier 2
Signed in, but no inbound external calls No number assigned, enterprise voice not enabled, or the licence lapsed. Check the account and the register before the device (§4.3 step 2). Tier 2
Shows the previous user’s name Reassigned without a clean sign-out, or a stale cached profile. Full sign-out, factory reset if needed, re-register as the new user, update the register. Tier 2
Dead — no display, no power PoE. Switch port, patch lead, or a port re-patched during other work. Test the port and the lead before the phone. Swap the phone last, not first. Tier 1
Poor audio, dropouts, one-way audio Network path or QoS, not the handset. Note time, both parties and direction, then Tier 3. A handset swap will not fix it and wastes a visit. Tier 3
Works, but emergency address is wrong or blank The phone moved, or the address was never set at assignment. Fix immediately (§8). This is not a cosmetic defect and it is not deferred to the next visit. Tier 2

5. Softphone or desk phone

The Teams client on a computer is a full telephone. It is free, it needs no firmware, it moves with the person, and it will never generate the ticket this document is named after. It is the default.

A desk phone is genuinely justified when A desk phone is not justified by
A position is covered by different people at different times — a front desk, a lab bench, a security post — and the phone belongs to the position rather than the person. Preference, habit, or having had one on the previous system.
The role must be reachable when the computer is off, asleep, or the person is away from the keyboard — Security, Facilities, plant rooms. Seniority. A desk phone is not a status object and treating it as one is how estates grow.
A headset is impractical or unsafe for the work — noisy environments, workshops, roles requiring situational awareness. “In case the computer fails.” If the network is down, so is the desk phone.
Accessibility. Where a physical handset is the accommodation, it is provided without argument and without a business case. A visitor-facing desk where a common-area device (§9) is the better and cheaper answer.
A public or shared location where anybody may need to make a call — lobby, elevator, meeting room (§9). A person who is on site two days a week.

Every desk phone is a capital item, an entry in a firmware estate, a physical thing that gets moved without telling anyone, and an emergency address that has to be kept true. That is the honest cost, and it is worth paying where the table’s left column applies and nowhere else.

6. Call queues and auto attendants

  • EVERY QUEUE AND EVERY ATTENDANT HAS A NAMED OWNER outside IT — a person in the department, not the department. The owner decides the greeting, the hours, the holiday schedule and who is in the queue. IT configures what the owner decides.
  • Each one runs on a resource account, which is a non-human identity governed by ACC-08: named, registered, no interactive sign-in, and reviewed with everything else.
  • An auto attendant with more than about five options, or more than two levels of menu, will not be used. Callers press zero, or hang up and email instead, which is worse for the department than a simpler menu would have been.
  • Every queue has an overflow destination and a timeout destination, and both are a human or a voicemail box somebody actually reads. A queue that overflows to another queue that overflows to nothing is a phone system that rings for four minutes and then hangs up on a prospective customer.
  • Holiday and closure schedules are set once a year, in advance, by the owner. An organization closed for a week with a greeting saying “our office hours are nine to five” is a small avoidable embarrassment.
  • Changes to greetings, hours and membership come from the owner and go through the service desk, so there is a record of who asked for what. Verbal changes to a published greeting are how a department’s number ends up saying something nobody approved.

The failure nobody reports

The characteristic failure of a call queue is that it routes to a queue with no members, or to a voicemail box nobody reads. It rings out to nothing, and the organization never hears about it — because the person who experienced it was outside the organization, and they did not raise a ticket. They just gave up.

THE CONTROL: a quarterly test call to every published number, every queue and every attendant, by Tier 2, with the result recorded. It takes under an hour for an estate this size and it is the only way this class of fault is ever found.

7. Voicemail and transcription

  • Voicemail is delivered to the user’s Exchange Online mailbox as a message with an audio attachment and a machine transcription. That single fact settles most questions about it.
  • VOICEMAIL IS MAIL. It is governed by M365-02, it falls under the mail retention floor (M365-16), it is discoverable, and it follows the mailbox at separation — so a mailbox converted to shared under M365-16 §4 carries the voicemail with it.
  • Machine transcription is often wrong, particularly with names, numbers and accents. The audio is the record; the transcript is a way of deciding whether to listen to it. Never quote a transcript as what somebody said.
  • A queue’s or a resource account’s voicemail goes to that account’s mailbox, which needs a named person who reads it (ACC-08, M365-03). A voicemail box nobody reads is worse than having no voicemail at all, because the caller reasonably believes they have left a message with the organization.
  • Users record their own greeting. A departmental greeting is the owner’s to record and to keep current, including at closures (§6).
  • Voicemail transcription can be disabled per user on request — occasionally the right answer where a role receives sensitive information by phone, since the transcript is text in a mailbox and is indexed as such (M365-15).

8. Emergency calling and dynamic emergency address

A softphone that reports the wrong location is worse than no phone at all.

If a member of staff dials 911 from Teams on a laptop and the call carries a static address registered against their number, responders are sent to that address. If the person has taken the laptop to another building, to a different site, or home, the address is wrong — and it is wrong with the full confidence of a system. Somebody who dialled 911 from their mobile would at least have been asked where they were.

This is a site safety matter, not a telephony configuration matter, and it is the section of this document that should be read by somebody outside IT.

NAMED OWNER: the network owner in IT owns the network location map (§8.2). Security owns the notification recipients and the response (§8.3). The Head of IT owns the compliance position (§8.1). Unowned, this section does not operate.

8.1 What the organization is required to do

  • KARI’S LAW requires that a multi-line telephone system permits 911 to be dialled directly, with no prefix or access code, from any device — and that a notification is sent to a designated on-site location when a 911 call is placed, so somebody at the site knows it happened.
  • RAY BAUM’S ACT requires that a dispatchable location is conveyed with the call — not just a street address, but enough to find the caller: building, floor, and room or equivalent where it can be determined.
  • Whether the organization’s current configuration satisfies both is a question IT should answer in writing rather than assume. It is prerequisite 2 in Appendix B, it belongs to the Head of IT, and where the answer is no it is a Tier 4 item with a date on it.
  • This document states the requirement, not a legal opinion on it. Where the answer is unclear, counsel and the carrier are the right people to ask, and asking is cheaper than the alternative.

8.2 Static and dynamic addresses

How it works Where it breaks
Static emergency address An address is registered against the number in the DID register and sent with every emergency call from it. The moment the device moves. Correct for a desk phone bolted to a location; wrong for any softphone, and dangerously plausible.
Dynamic emergency address The client’s location is determined from the network it is on — subnet, wireless access point, switch and port, or a network site — and the matching civic address is sent. It requires a network location map to exist and to be maintained. Without one, the platform has nothing to look the location up in and falls back to the static address.
Off site Teams cannot determine a location on an unknown network. A user may be prompted to confirm an address, which they may not do or may do wrongly. This is the case with no technical fix. It is fixed by telling people, in plain words, in the end-user guidance and in this document.

THE RULE FOR USERS, and it belongs in every end-user guide the organization publishes: if you are not on site, Teams may not know where you are. In an emergency, dial 911 from your mobile phone. Nobody has ever regretted that instruction.

8.3 What has to be built and maintained

  1. A network location map: every site subnet, wireless access point and switch port mapped to a civic address with a building and, where possible, a floor. This is real work, it belongs to whoever owns the network, and it is the piece that is usually never done because it is nobody’s single job.
  2. An emergency address on every number in the register, set at assignment and confirmed at every §4 re-registration (§4.3 step 10).
  3. Emergency call notification to Security, so that when 911 is dialled from an organization device the security desk is told immediately and can meet responders at the right door. Security names the recipients and confirms they are monitored.
  4. A test, annually, with Security and with the carrier, using the carrier’s test procedure and never by dialling 911 speculatively. Record the result; an untested emergency configuration is an assumption.
  5. A re-test whenever the network changes in a way that moves subnets, access points or switches. This is why §8 is in the review trigger in the control block.

9. Shared area and common-area phones

  • A shared phone signs in as a RESOURCE ACCOUNT, never as a member of staff (ACC-08). A personal account on a shared handset means every call from a public lobby is attributed to one individual, and it means that person’s sign-in sits on a device anybody can reach.
  • Its physical location is recorded in the DID register and IS its emergency address. That is the primary reason the device exists in many locations, and it is the field to check first when the phone is moved.
  • No voicemail on a common-area phone unless a named person reads it (§7). A lobby phone with an unread voicemail box is a promise the organization is not keeping.
  • Lobby, elevator, laboratory and meeting room phones are inventoried and included in the quarterly test call (§6). Nobody reports a broken phone in an elevator until the day it matters, and on that day it matters a great deal.
  • Meeting room and conference-room telephony sits alongside the AV standard — see DEV-18, which covers the room rather than the line.

10. What happens to a number at separation

  • The number belongs to the organization, not to the person. It is unassigned as part of the leaver procedure (ACC-01 §7.2), on the same day as everything else, and the register is updated at that moment rather than at the next reconciliation.
  • QUARANTINE BEFORE REASSIGNMENT: recommended 90 days. Reassigning a number the week after somebody leaves means their successor receives calls intended for them — from external contacts who have no idea — which is awkward at best and a disclosure at worst.
  • Voicemail follows the mailbox (§7). It is not separately deleted, and it is not separately preserved either; the mailbox disposition under ACC-12 and M365-16 §4 governs it.
  • Where the number was PUBLISHED — printed, on a web page, in a directory — it does not follow a person at all. It moves to a queue or a resource account so that callers reach the function (§6). This is worth arranging before the departure rather than after.
  • The emergency address record is cleared when the number is unassigned and set again when it is reassigned. A quarantined number with a live emergency address is a wrong answer waiting to be given.
  • The licence is reclaimed with the rest (M365-20), and a desk phone the person held goes back to the pool under DEV-15 — signed out, factory reset, and its register entry cleared.

Appendix B — Prerequisites, with owners

This document describes procedures that depend on four artefacts. Two of them probably do not exist today. Until they do, the sections that rest on them operate on memory, which is how a desk-phone ticket ages for months. Each has a number, an owner and a first deliverable.

# Prerequisite Owner What it takes, and what depends on it
1 The DID register Network owner, IT Export the numbers from the Teams admin centre and from the carrier, reconcile them, and fill in the §3.1 columns — including the emergency address for each. Perhaps a day. §3, §8, §10 and the whole of Appendix A depend on it. Nothing else in this document should be scheduled ahead of it.
2 The emergency calling compliance position Head of IT, with the carrier and, where needed, counsel A written answer to one question: does the organization’s current configuration satisfy direct 911 dialling, on-site notification, and dispatchable location (§8.1)? Where the answer is no or unknown, it becomes a Tier 4 item with a date. This is a site safety matter and it should not wait on the rest of the document.
3 The network location map Network owner, IT, with Security confirming the civic addresses Site subnets, wireless access points and switch ports mapped to building and floor. Larger than prerequisite 1 and the thing that makes dynamic emergency addressing possible at all (§8.2). Where it cannot be completed quickly, the interim position is that softphone users are told in plain words to use a mobile phone off site.
4 The resource account inventory and firmware baseline Tier 2 Every queue, attendant and common-area device with its resource account (ACC-08) and its named human owner; every handset with its model and firmware version (DEV-01). Enables §6, §9, remote sign-in at §4.2, and the quarterly test call.

Recommended sequence: 1, then 2, then 4, then 3. Prerequisite 2 is second not because it is easiest but because it is the one where being wrong has consequences the organization cannot undo.
